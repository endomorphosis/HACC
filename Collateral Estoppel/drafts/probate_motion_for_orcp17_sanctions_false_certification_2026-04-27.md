## II. Procedural Authority

ORS 125.050 provides that, except as otherwise provided by ORS chapter 125, the Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure and Oregon Evidence Code apply in protective proceedings.

ORS 125.075 allows any person interested in the affairs or welfare of a respondent or protected person, including persons entitled to notice under ORS 125.060, to present objections to a petition or motion in a protective proceeding.

ORCP 17 C provides that a person who signs, files, or submits a pleading, motion, or argument certifies that the certifications are based on reasonable knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances. ORCP 17 C(2) prohibits improper-purpose filings. ORCP 17 C(3) requires legal positions to be warranted by existing law or a nonfrivolous legal argument. ORCP 17 C(4) requires factual assertions to be supported by evidence unless specifically identified as expected to be supported after further investigation or discovery.

ORCP 17 D(1) permits sanctions against a person, party, or attorney responsible for a false certification after notice and an opportunity to be heard. ORCP 17 D(3) requires a sanctions motion to be made separately, to describe the alleged false certification with specificity, and prevents sanctions if the challenged paper or argument is corrected within 21 days after service. ORCP 17 D(4) limits sanctions to amounts sufficient to reimburse attorney fees and other expenses incurred by reason of the false certification, and, on clear and convincing evidence of wanton misconduct, amounts sufficient to deter future false certifications. ORCP 17 D(5) requires any sanctions order to specifically describe the false certification and the grounds for the sanction.

ORS 125.095 permits payment of reasonable fees, costs, and disbursements from the funds of a person subject to a protective proceeding only as authorized in a protective proceeding. ORS 125.098 requires the Court to consider statutory fee factors when deciding whether fees should be paid from the respondent's or protected person's funds, including whether the services benefited the person subject to the protective proceeding.

ORS 40.135 / OEC 311 recognizes a presumption that willfully suppressed evidence would be adverse to the party suppressing it. Objector invokes that presumption only if the Court finds willful suppression or nonproduction of records material to the challenged petition allegations.

## V. Improper Purpose And Reasonable Inquiry

Objector contends the challenged petition was not merely inaccurate in isolated particulars. The record supports a reasonable inference, subject to hearing proof, that the petition was presented or maintained for an improper purpose: to bypass the pending restraining-order case, obtain practical control over Jane through probate, and shift blame for housing delays to Benjamin while omitting Solomon's own disputed role and the existing protective-order framework.

At minimum, the challenged allegations required reasonable inquiry before Solomon and counsel sought waiver of notice, emergency appointment, plenary authority, fees from Jane's funds, and writ-based law-enforcement transport. The petition should have identified allegations that still required investigation rather than presenting them as established facts.

The Court does not need to decide criminal perjury to grant ORCP 17 relief. The issue is whether the petition's certifications were false under ORCP 17 C and whether corrective or deterrent sanctions are warranted under ORCP 17 D after notice and hearing.
